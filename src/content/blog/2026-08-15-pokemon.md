2026 is the year of agents, with many people actively experimenting with building agentic workflows to integrate into both professional and personal projects.  However, many of the public guides and example projects have been lackluster.  People are taught to rely on cloud models, and to just throw data and information at the model until it solves it.  Many tooling guides focus on how to do things like MCP, but not the why of which things in your workflow should be turned into a tool call, or how that tool call looks like in a local environement.  We built AI Village Plays Pokemon as a fun way to solve this gap, by using the game of Pokemon as an example of how to build a custom harness and tools for local models.  

First, lets start with some basic definations.  The harness is the wraper code that calls the LLM each cycle of the agent.  A good harness dynamically handles the context being presented to the model, and keeps track of what tools should and should not be presented to the model, as well as what to explain about it's previous actions.  Tools are external programs that the model can call to perform a specific deterministc action or solve a discrete problem.  We're going to keep it simple and ignore things like skills and RAG calls for now.  

You might notice that we have our harness be responsible for remembering what the model did before and needs to do next rather than having the model just handle this itself with it's own memory.  The reason for this is that because we are focusing on local models, we are also responsible for the speed and performance of the model, and local models run faster the smaller you can make the context they are processing per run.  By offloading this to the harness, we are able to keep our total context being passed to the model fairly small, and thus allow the game to be played at a relatively normal pace even for models running on laptops or older / lower power hardware.  

We're also being a little weird with how we define a tool.  Normally a tool is just described as an API wraper, ususally exposing something like a FastAPI or OpenAPI compatable endpoint to the model in a way that it can request something be called or returned.  Thats because since most agents are cloud based, most of the tools they deal with are running ontop of some sort of web framework, and you are having to play in that space for security and accessablility concerns.  However we are building everyling locally, and building all the tools explictly for our models, so rather than worry about permissions and the like, we can focus on what parts of the game make sense to be considered tools, and which ones do not.  

Perhaps the most obvious part of the game to turn into a tool are the battles themselves, or at least the damage calculator.  At the end of the day, battles in Pokemon are math formulas with little guys, and each generation has that formula slightly change as new pokemon types and abilities are added, and indvidual moves change their typing and effects.  In our case, we are playing pokemon LeafGreen, which is a remake of the original Generation One game of Pokemon Green (or Blue for those of us in the US).  However because the remake was built using the Generation Three engine, it uses the Generation 3 damage calculator instead of the Generation One.  This means that Steel and Dark types now exist, and moves are considered Special or Physical attacks based their typing.  While this sounds like far too much info for what is at the end  of the day a basic formula, this is why it makes since to have damage calculation be a tool instead of something the model decides for itself.  Having the model try and guess how much damage a move will do, or which move is most effective burns a lot of tokens, and makes the system prone to hallucinations.  Since the actual formula never changes, and instead it's just a case of putting in both yours and your opponents stats and moves, and ranking the results.  This is an example of a discrete or algorthimic tool, since you are giving the model a way to relably solve a specific problem.
